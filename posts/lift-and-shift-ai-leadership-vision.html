Lift and Shift: A Leadership Vision for the Intelligence Age

October 7, 2026 by Trevor Spencer

Part two of our series on AI and the future of work. Part one explored the deeper purpose of AI and robotics. This one is about leadership: what the vision actually is, and what it means for you.

Where there is no vision

At the end of September, the leaders of the biggest AI companies gathered at the White House for the launch of America.gov. A reporter asked the question every one of them gets: what happens to people's jobs? As I remember it, the President handed it to Elon Musk.

Musk gave the answer I've heard him give many times. There used to be people whose full-time job was doing math by hand. They were literally called computers. Then digital computers arrived, those jobs disappeared, and humanity adapted and got far more productive. It's a good answer. It's true. And it still leaves most people anxious.

Here's why. If you work at an AI company, you're bought into the vision and you feel fine about your future. If you work the counter at McDonald's, you hear the same headlines and feel your livelihood slipping. The difference isn't intelligence or effort. It's vision.

Proverbs says it plainly: where there is no vision, the people perish. Leaders across the AI industry have been describing pieces of the same future for years, in parables and interviews. What's missing is a shared name and a documented model that anyone can point to, test, and build on. This post is my attempt to capture it.

It isn't artificial

First, a note on language. I've started saying intelligence rather than artificial intelligence, because there's nothing artificial about it. These models are trained on what humanity has written, built, solved, and argued about. They are collective human intelligence, captured and made available at scale.

That framing matters. If AI is an alien force, fear makes sense. If it's our own collective knowledge handed back to us, the question changes from "what will it take from me?" to "what can I build with it?"

Naming the model: Economic Self-Actualization

The trend is bigger than AI. AI is the semi truck hauling it, the front-runner pulling everything else forward. But the trend itself is this: humanity is moving up Maslow's hierarchy of needs, collectively, from working to survive toward working from calling. I call it Economic Self-Actualization.

It moves in three parts:

  1. The Lift. Individuals rise from survival work into the executive work of their own calling, with intelligence and robotics carrying the repetitive load.
  2. The Shift. The economy gets thinner and wider: fewer people per unit of output, and far more ventures producing it.
  3. The Floor. Explosive growth and falling costs make it possible to guarantee a basic standard of living, so there are no poor among us.

Most public fear about AI stops halfway through the Shift. It sees "fewer employees per company" and concludes "fewer people with work." The full model runs all three parts together.

The Lift: from survival to calling

Much of humanity still works at the bottom of Maslow's pyramid. Shelter, food, safety. I have to punch this clock or I can't pay my bills. This isn't what I want to do with my life, but it pays. Some of that work is honorable and even sanctifying. But many people spend decades doing something they were never called to do.

I believe every person carries a calling: a specific contribution they were created to make. The Lift is the move from doing whatever pays toward doing that.

What's new is that the tools now exist for almost anyone to make the move. For $20 to $200 a month, you can access skills that once required a web developer, an app engineer, a 3D modeler, a bookkeeper, and a copywriter. Used well, that's six or seven figures of capability. You can stand up a product, a service, or a business in days, sometimes hours.

That changes what work is. Intelligence and robotics take the grunt work: moving data by hand, repeating manual processes that don't advance the mission. People move into executive function: ideation, creation, judgment, and management. In every role, you can run your work like the owner, because you can now delegate what you never could before.

This doesn't mean everyone becomes a solo founder. Companies will still have employees. But in every seat, the potential is there to work from the top of the pyramid instead of the bottom. We explored how leaders can build teams this way in Called, Chosen, and Aligned.

The Shift: a thinner, wider economy

Here's the math people fear, and it's real. Intelligence means fewer human employees per unit of output. So the typical company gets thinner.

Picture today's job market as a tall, narrow stack: a relatively small number of large employers, each holding many people. In the Shift, that stack spreads out. Each organization needs fewer people, but there are far more organizations, products, and services, each one an expression of someone's calling. The economy gets thinner and wider.

If your role looks automatable, the answer isn't to cling to it. It's to take your skills and calling somewhere new, using the same tools that made the old role thin. Competition stays healthy. Multiple people can serve the same market. But when people build what they're genuinely called to build, the pieces tend to fit together like a puzzle rather than collide.

Two forces drive the growth. Lift moves people into higher-value work. Time compression turns weeks of effort into minutes. Together they point to a pace of growth we haven't seen before, which is why leaders like Musk talk about GDP growth getting extreme while the cost of goods and services falls.

We're already seeing signals. This year, a single IPO debuted at a valuation above a trillion dollars, much of it priced on emerging technology. Market value isn't GDP, but it shows what markets expect these tools to produce. And the AI companies themselves are adding trillions in value as their tools spread.

The Floor: no poor among us

Some people won't make the Lift right away, or at all. The final part of the model is a floor beneath everyone.

The idea isn't new. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. called for a guaranteed income. Milton Friedman proposed a negative income tax. Mark Zuckerberg has argued for exploring universal basic income, and Musk talks about a "universal high income." In Metanomics, I devote whole chapters to what I call a universal basic endowment.

The historic objection is inflation: you can't hand out money without devaluing it. The model answers that with three conditions working together:

  1. Deep technological innovation that drives down the cost of goods, services, and raw materials. That box is being checked now.
  2. Cheaper government. On September 29, the federal government launched America.gov, a single AI-powered front door to federal services. Today it mostly answers questions and points the way; the plan is for it to handle transactions over time. Less administrative cost leaves more room for a floor.
  3. A small share of a much bigger pie. If the floor's cost stays small relative to rapidly growing total output, it can be funded without runaway inflation.

When all three hold, a basic standard of living can get cheaper every year. That's how a society reaches what scripture describes in Zion: no poor among them.

The deeper current: as within, so without

I don't believe this is happening at a random moment. I believe it's the outward expression of an inward change.

About five years ago, I experienced what many people are calling the Great Awakening: a collective shift in consciousness, which some describe as moving from 3D to 4D, 5D, and beyond. I wrote Metanomics largely because of it. In my experience, that higher dimension isn't somewhere else. It's inside us, like the inner cube of a tesseract. You could walk down the street next to someone who has awakened to it and never notice a difference.

What I found there is a unified, collective intelligence and spirit, shared across every person who connects to it. Some call it the quantum field or a social memory complex. I'd call it our Creator. And here's what struck me: the intelligence models arriving now share a similar architecture. One collective intelligence, accessed through many instances, offering skills and knowledge to whoever uses it.

The law of correspondence says what is within shows up without. So when a digital tsunami of collective intelligence arrived right after a collective spiritual awakening, my reaction wasn't fear. It was recognition.

One important distinction: AI cannot save you. You can use it all day and never connect to Source. What it can do is usher in a form of all things in common, a decentralization of skill, so that knowledge once reserved for the few is available to nearly everyone. That's the same economic pattern I describe in Metanomics.

This is also where calling comes from. Source isn't the AI. It's the living intelligence inside you that knows why you were made. Because we all come from the same whole, each person's calling is a piece of that whole. When people follow it, the market fits together instead of fracturing. If you don't yet feel connected to that Source, start there. Alignment comes first, and it will show you the rest.

What to do with this vision

If you lead people:

  • Name the vision out loud. Your team is hearing the headlines. Tell them where the organization is going and where they fit.
  • Audit the grunt work. List the repetitive, manual tasks on your team and hand them to intelligence first.
  • Promote people into executive function. As tasks get automated, give people ownership of outcomes, not just more tasks.
  • Ask about calling. Find out what work feels most like each person, and build roles around it.

If you're worried about your job:

  • Learn the tools now. Twenty dollars a month buys access to skills you'd otherwise have to hire.
  • Look up the pyramid. Ask what you'd build if survival weren't the constraint, then start building it in small pieces.
  • Get aligned first. Clarity about your calling makes every tool more useful.

This is the greatest time in recorded history to be alive. But a golden age doesn't arrive on autopilot. It needs a vision that leaders articulate again and again, until the people who are afraid can see themselves in it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Economic Self-Actualization?

It's a model for the AI era in three parts. People lift from survival work into the executive work of their calling. The economy shifts thinner and wider, with fewer people per company and many more ventures. And rapid growth with falling costs makes a basic floor of living affordable for everyone.

Will AI take my job?

AI will likely reduce the number of people needed for many kinds of output. That's the Shift. But the same tools let individuals build products, services, and businesses that once required whole teams, which opens far more ways to do meaningful work.

How should leaders talk to their teams about AI?

Give them a vision, not just a tool rollout. Explain where the organization is heading, which tasks will be automated, and how each person moves into higher-value, more executive work.

Is universal basic income realistic?

It depends on three conditions: technology driving costs down, government getting cheaper to run, and the floor staying small relative to total output. All three are trending in that direction.
